Every fall, a kid who can dribble circles around the field gets cut at tryouts for a reason that has nothing to do with skill: they can't change direction fast enough. Coaches call it first-step quickness, and it's one of the easiest things to improve in the two or three weeks before tryouts start — easier than shooting, easier than passing, easier than anything that takes years to build.

The drill worth running is the star drill. Set one cone in the middle of a driveway or a patch of grass, then four more cones about five yards out in a plus shape — front, back, left, right. Starting at the center cone, sprint to each of the four, touch the ground, and sprint back to center before moving to the next one. A full lap through all four takes maybe twenty seconds. Rest, then go again. Five reps is a real workout; five minutes total is enough to notice a difference within two weeks. No cones in the garage is fine too — a chalk mark, a shoe, or a stick laid on the grass holds the spot just as well.

Four or five times a week is plenty. This isn't a training program that needs a coach standing over it every day, just a habit slipped in before dinner or after the dog gets walked. Kids get bored of straight sprints fast; a pattern with a shape to it holds their attention longer, which matters more than it sounds like it should.

## Why does this matter more than practicing the sport itself?

Most kids trying out already know how to kick, dribble, or catch — that's not usually what separates who makes the team. What a coach is watching for in the first few minutes of a tryout is how fast a kid plants a foot and goes the other way, because that's the skill that shows up on every single play regardless of position. It's also the one most families never train on purpose, since it doesn't show up in casual practice the way shooting or passing does.

## What if my kid has never done agility work before?

Start slower than feels necessary. The first session or two, walk through the pattern before sprinting it, so the footwork is right before the speed gets added. A kid who's never done a drill like this will still see real improvement inside two weeks, because change-of-direction speed responds fast — unlike strength or raw skill, which take a season to build, this is closer to a habit.
