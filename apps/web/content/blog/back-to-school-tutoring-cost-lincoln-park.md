It's the first week of August, and somewhere in a Lincoln Park kitchen a parent has just handed a rising sixth grader a summer workbook that hasn't been touched since June. School starts back up in a few weeks, and the gap between "I used to get this" and "I remember how to do this" is exactly what a handful of sessions with a tutor can close before the first bell rings.

The question right behind "should we get a tutor" is "what's this going to cost." Tutors on College Crew list between $23 and $30 an hour — not a full test-prep program with a curriculum and a contract, just someone patient who remembers how the material works and can explain it a second way when the first way doesn't land.

## What actually changes the price?

Subject and grade level matter less than you would expect. Providers set their own hourly rates on College Crew, so what you pay depends more on which student you book than on whether it is third-grade multiplication or high-school algebra. How often you book changes the total rather than the rate — a standing weekly slot for the semester and a one-time refresher before school starts cost the same per hour.

One thing that doesn't change: hourly work through College Crew has a one-hour minimum, billed in 15-minute increments after that. A 45-minute session that runs a little long doesn't round up to a full extra hour — it rounds up to the next quarter.

## When should you actually book?

Before the first day, if you are going to book at all. Tutors on College Crew set their own weekly availability — a student might have Tuesday and Thursday afternoons open and nothing else — so the earlier you look, the more of those windows you have to choose from. Bookings also need at least 12 hours' notice, which means the night before a quiz is already too late.

## What should you ask before the first session?

Every student on College Crew goes through the [same verification](/about/customers) before they ever show up on [Browse](/browse?service=tutoring) — 18+, a real `.edu` email, a manual student-ID check, and founder approval. What verification doesn't guarantee is subject fit, so read what the tutor actually studied and what they've tutored before, not just their star rating — a physics major who's tutored middle school algebra for two years is a safer bet for your sixth grader's fractions than a first-year undecided who's never taught anyone. It helps to be specific in your first message, too: which subject, which grade, and whether it's "keep up with the school year" or "catch up on something specific from last year." A tutor who knows that before the first session shows up prepared instead of improvising.

None of this has to be complicated. A kid who's rusty in August doesn't need a program — they need one person who's patient, available on a Tuesday afternoon, and actually remembers how the material works. That's a smaller ask than it sounds like in July, and a much smaller one than it sounds like in October.
