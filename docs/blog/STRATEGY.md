# Blog strategy

The weekly blog routine reads this file to decide what to write and how to write
it. It starts every run with **zero context** — nothing here is background
reading, it is the whole brief.

Related: [`PUBLISHING.md`](./PUBLISHING.md) (how a post ships),
[`published.md`](./published.md) (what has already been written).

---

## Who the posts are for

**Homeowners and parents in the pilot service areas: the North Shore and
Lincoln Park.** People who have a task they'd rather not do, could pay someone
to do it, and are trying to work out what it costs and who to trust.

Not students. Not investors. Not other founders. When a topic could be angled at
either the person hiring or the student earning, **write it for the person
hiring.**

## What College Crew actually is

Facts a post may state, because they are true:

- A curated, hyperlocal marketplace connecting neighbors with **verified college
  students (18+)** for home and household services.
- Verification is real and specific: an 18+ date-of-birth gate, `.edu` email,
  manual student-ID review, and founder approval before a provider appears.
- Live in the **North Shore and Lincoln Park** during the pilot.
- Customers browse, book, and pay in the app. Providers are independent.
- Hourly work has a one-hour minimum and 15-minute increments; some
  visual-scope jobs use a single fixed quote instead.

## Choosing the week's topic

Read [`published.md`](./published.md) first. **Never repeat a topic already
listed there**, and don't write a near-duplicate of one (a "spring lawn cleanup"
post and a "spring yard prep" post are the same post).

Then pick the highest-value gap, weighing in this order:

1. **Seasonal fit.** A leaf-cleanup post in October beats an evergreen one. Check
   today's date and write what a neighbor is actually thinking about this month.
2. **Search intent that converts.** Cost and "should I hire this out" questions
   beat general interest.
3. **Service coverage.** Prefer a service with no post yet over a fourth post
   about dog walking.

⚠ **The rotation rule below outranks all three.** Left alone, this list picks a
broad cost guide every single week, because a broad cost guide always scores
highest on it. Choose the shape first, then apply this list *within* that shape.

### Two axes: what the post is, and how wide it aims

Every post has a **type** and a **breadth**, and both should vary week to week.
An archive of nothing but broad cost guides competes for a handful of crowded
phrases and wins none of them. The long tail is where a new site actually wins:
"how do I get hard water spots off glass" has one good answer and almost nobody
competing to give it.

| Type | Breadth | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | broad | What drives the price, what to expect, what to ask | "What does it cost to get your windows washed in Lincoln Park?" |
| **Seasonal** | broad | What needs doing this month and what's worth hiring out | "The five yard jobs worth doing before the first frost" |
| **Hiring guide** | broad | How to pick someone, what to ask, what good looks like | "What to ask before you hand someone your spare key" |
| **Comparison** | broad | Honest options including ones that aren't us | "Hiring a neighbor's kid vs. a service: what actually differs" |
| **How-to / method** | niche | One genuinely good technique, explained properly | "One trick that gets streaks off a west-facing window" |
| **Specific problem** | niche | A single annoying thing and what fixes it | "How to get hard water spots off glass" |
| **Edge case** | niche | The awkward situation nobody else writes about | "What to do with a couch you can't get down the stairs" |

The comparison post matters more than it looks. A post that names real
alternatives honestly reads as credible to both people and answer engines; one
that pretends College Crew is the only option reads as an ad and gets treated
like one.

### The rotation rule

Read the **Shape** column in [`published.md`](./published.md) and obey both:

- **At least two of any four consecutive posts are niche.**
- **Never the same type twice in a row.**

If the last two posts were broad, this week is niche. That is a rule, not a
preference — left to judgment, every week looks like a cost guide, because a
cost guide always seems like the highest-value gap. Record the shape you chose
in the log so next week's run can apply the same test.

### Niche posts inspire — they never specify the service

A niche post is **one good idea worth trying**, not a description of what a
booking gets you. That distinction is load-bearing. Lay out a six-step window
method and a customer can reasonably expect those six steps; the student who
shows up never agreed to them. "Five fun things to do while babysitting" reads
to a parent as the itinerary. Neither post meant to promise anything, and both
did.

How to stay on the right side of it:

- **One idea, not the method.** "One trick worth trying on a west-facing
  window", not "the right way to wash a window". Indefinite, offered, singular.
  A headline may reach further than the body delivers — "the best way to…" is
  fine as a title, and must not come back in the body as our standard procedure.
- **Watch the grammatical subject.** Write about the task or about the reader,
  never about our students performing it. *"A squeegee beats a spray bottle"* is
  knowledge. *"Our students use a squeegee"* is a spec sheet. The failure hides
  in one word.
- **Never describe what a booking includes.** Scope lives in each provider's own
  listing, because each provider sets it. A post that enumerates it is writing a
  contract on their behalf.
- **Close open.** "If you'd rather not spend Saturday on it, you can book
  someone" plus the link — then stop. Not what they'll bring, not what they'll
  do, not how long it takes.

⚠ **Never write the disclaimer.** No "results may vary", no "this isn't a
guarantee", no "we can't promise", no "of course, every home is different". A
promise named in the negative is still the reader thinking about promises, it
reads defensively, and it is the tell that gives the whole thing away. You avoid
making a promise by not making one, not by announcing that you haven't.

The FAQ is where this leaks most, because FAQ questions invite exactly the wrong
answer. *"What does a babysitter actually do?"* answered as a task list is a
scope promise. Answered as "you describe what you need when you book, and
providers set their own rates and availability" it is how the marketplace works
— true, checkable, and ours to say.

## The shape of a post

**At least 700 words, with no ceiling — but every additional word has to earn
its place structurally, not by padding.** The reason is in
[SEO and GEO](#seo-and-geo) below: an AI answer engine retrieves and cites
*passages*, not pages, so a longer post is not a longer page. It is more
separate chances to be the passage that gets quoted.

That gives one hard test for length:

> **A post grows by adding a section that answers a distinct question. It never
> grows by stretching a section that is already finished.**

A broad guide with six real questions to answer runs long and should. A
specific-problem post that genuinely answers its question in 600 words stops at
600. If a section does not answer a question a person would actually type, cut
it — do not expand it to look substantial. **Padding is itself an AI tell**, and
a reader who feels a post repeating itself does not finish it.

1. **Open concrete.** A specific moment, season, or situation — not a definition
   and not a throat-clear. **Vary the opening move:** a season, a thing that
   went wrong, the question asked plainly, a single object. Four posts in a row
   that open on a sidewalk scene read as one post written four times. Never open
   with "In today's world" or "As a homeowner, you know that…".
2. **The middle is a sequence of self-contained answers.** This is the part that
   ranks and the part that gets cited. Each section takes one question and
   finishes it. Be specific enough to be useful to someone who will never hire
   us. Structure it per [SEO and GEO](#seo-and-geo) — that section is where the
   real requirements live.
3. **Subheads follow the content, not a quota, and not one formula.** Usually
   three to six now that posts run longer. **At least one must be phrased as the
   exact question a person would type**, because that is what an answer engine
   matches and deep-links. The rest should not all be questions — two drafts in
   a row have opened every single subhead with an interrogative, and three
   question subheads stacked in a row read as a template, not a person. Mix in a
   plain noun phrase or a flat statement. Never repeat the same opening word
   across two subheads in one post.
4. **Close short.** Two or three sentences. No hard sell, no "In conclusion", no
   "Ready to get started?".

### Required in every post

- **3–5 FAQ entries** in the frontmatter. Real questions, answered in 1–3
  sentences each — a complete answer that stands alone when quoted, because
  being quoted out of context is exactly what happens. This is the single
  highest-leverage thing in the post for AI answer engines.

  ⚠ **An FAQ entry may not restate a section of the body.** It is extra surface,
  not a summary. Both drafts written so far wasted it: the tutoring post's FAQ
  re-asked its own subheads, and the drill post's first answer repeated a body
  sentence nearly word for word. A duplicated answer gets the `FAQPage` markup
  discounted and buys no new coverage. Point each entry at a question the body
  does **not** already take on — the smaller, odder, more specific the better.
- **`tags`** — the relevant service slug from the table above **first**, then at
  most two more. These become `keywords` in the post's structured data, so a
  freeform tag nobody uses elsewhere is wasted. Put the tags in the canvas so
  they are Gianna's to change, not invented at publish time a week later.
- **2–3 internal links** in the body, woven into sentences. At least one to a
  relevant `/browse?service=<slug>`, and **at least one to another published
  post** whenever `published.md` holds one on a related topic. Never a bare
  "click here".

  ⚠ **Vary the anchor text.** The first two drafts both linked `/about/customers`
  on the words "same verification". Repeating one anchor across an archive
  teaches a search engine that the phrase and the page are interchangeable, and
  it reads as boilerplate to a person, because it is.

  ⚠ **Every internal link must point at a page that actually exists.** A
  confident link to a page we never built is a 404 in published marketing, and
  it is the kind of error nobody notices until a customer hits it. Link only to
  these:

  | Path | What it is |
  |---|---|
  | `/` | Home |
  | `/browse` and `/browse?service=<slug>` | Browse, optionally filtered — slugs in the table above |
  | `/about`, `/about/customers`, `/about/students` | About pages |
  | `/blog` and `/blog/<slug>` | The blog, and any post listed in `published.md` as `published` |
  | `/faq` | FAQ |
  | `/support` | Support form |
  | `/legal`, `/privacy` | Legal |

  Nothing else. There is no `/pricing`, no `/how-it-works`, no `/services`, no
  per-service landing page. If the sentence wants one, rewrite the sentence.
  Confirm anything you are unsure of by looking for the matching
  `apps/web/app/**/page.tsx` before you link it.
- **1–2 outbound links to a named, checkable authority**, where the post has a
  fact worth sourcing. See [Cite outward](#cite-outward) — this is a
  requirement, not a nicety, and neither post written so far has a single one.

  The allowlist above governs **internal** links only; it has nothing to say
  about where you cite outward, which is by definition off this site. What
  governs those is that the source is real, named in the sentence, and something
  anyone could check.
- **A meta `description`** of roughly 150 characters that reads as a promise of
  what the post answers, not a summary of what it contains.
- **`imageAlt`** describing the photograph, not restating the title.

## SEO and GEO

Two things read every post: a person who searched, and a model assembling an
answer for someone who never sees our site. Most of the work serves both. Where
it doesn't, this section says so.

The site is already set up for this end — `app/robots.ts` deliberately admits
GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ClaudeBot, Claude-SearchBot and PerplexityBot, and every
post ships `BlogPosting`, `BreadcrumbList` and `FAQPage` structured data with an
`id` on every heading. None of that needs thinking about. What follows is the
part only the writing can do.

### An answer engine retrieves passages, not pages

This is the fact that changes how a post is built. Generative search embeds a
page in chunks, retrieves the chunks that match a question, and cites the few it
actually leaned on. A passage earns a citation when it carries a complete unit
of information — the claim, the evidence for it, and the edge of what it covers
— in roughly 150 to 300 words. Past about 200 words, precision drops when two
different intents share one block.

So a long post is not a long page. **It is a set of separate chances to be the
passage that gets quoted.** Six sections that each finish one question give six
openings; the same word count as one undifferentiated essay gives roughly one.

That is the whole argument for the length rule above, and the whole reason
padding is worse than useless: a stretched section blurs the intent of the chunk
it lives in and makes it *less* retrievable, not more.

**One question per section. Finish it before the next subhead.**

### Self-containment is semantic, not dimensional

⚠ **Read this one carefully — it is where the two halves of this file can be
made to fight.**

A section has to make sense lifted out with nothing around it, because that is
exactly how it will be used. In practice that means one habit:

- **Name the subject again in the first sentence of each section.** No "this",
  "it", "that approach", or "the drill above" reaching back across a heading. A
  pronoun whose antecedent lives in the previous section turns a quotable
  passage into a fragment.

It does **not** mean sections should be the same size, the same shape, or built
to a word count. Chunking applied mechanically produces uniform mid-length
blocks in an even cadence, which is precisely the texture
[the humanizer skill](#sounding-like-a-person) exists to destroy. Semantic
self-containment and varied rhythm are compatible: it is the *boundary* between
sections that stays clean, not the size of them. A two-sentence section next to
a two-hundred-word one is good writing and costs nothing in retrieval.

### Answer first, then explain

The first sentence or two after a subhead must answer the subhead completely
enough to stand alone. Then explain, qualify, and give the detail.

This is the same discipline as the FAQ, applied to the body. It is also the
opposite of the usual instinct, which is to build toward the answer — the drill
post put its answer in the third clause of the second sentence, where nothing
can lift it.

It agrees with the humanizer skill's §29: the sentence after a heading should do
real work, not restate the heading as a warm-up.

### Cite outward

**This is the highest-leverage change in this document, and the one that looks
at first like it violates the honesty rules.**

The founding GEO study (Aggarwal et al., ACM SIGKDD 2024, ~10,000 queries)
measured which edits actually raise a source's odds of being cited. Adding
statistics scored about **+41%** and adding quotations and cited sources about
**+37%**. Keyword stuffing did nothing at all.

Meanwhile [Honesty rules](#honesty-rules--these-are-hard-limits) call an invented
statistic the single worst failure this routine can produce. Both are correct.
The resolution is one word: **outward**.

The existing test — *who could check it?* — already draws the line exactly. It
has only ever been pointed at what to refuse. Point it the other way and it also
says what to reach for:

| Anyone could check it → **cite it, by name** | Only we could check it, and we haven't → **never** |
|---|---|
| The National Weather Service's average first-frost date for Chicago | What our providers charge |
| A city ordinance on yard-waste pickup | How fast slots fill |
| A school district's published academic calendar | What "most" neighbors pay |
| A manufacturer's spec, or a published study | Demand, scarcity, popularity |

Two rules for using one:

- **Name the source inside the sentence**, not just in the link. The shape to
  copy is *"The National Weather Service puts Chicago's average first frost at
  <the date it actually gives>"* — a passage that still carries its attribution
  after someone quotes it. *"Frost usually arrives around then ([source](#))"*
  does not: the attribution lives in the markup, and the markup is the first
  thing to go when a passage gets lifted. Lifted is the only way it will ever be
  read.

  ⚠ Look the number up. The example above is deliberately left blank because
  **this file does not know Chicago's frost date either**, and a date copied out
  of an illustration is an invented fact wearing a real source's name.
- **Only cite what you actually know to be true.** A fabricated citation is
  worse than an uncited claim, because it looks verified. If you are not
  confident the fact and the source are real, leave a
  `[NEEDS REAL SOURCE: …]` marker instead. The marker rules apply here in full.

Neither post written so far cites anything external. That is the largest single
gap between what these posts are and what gets quoted.

### Name the specific thing

Models match on entities. Vague nouns retrieve badly and read like filler; named
things retrieve well and read like someone who knows the job.

Write *the star drill*, not "an agility exercise". *Hard water spots*, not
"mineral buildup issues". *First-step quickness*, *Lincoln Park*, *the North
Shore*, the actual sport, the actual month. The drill post got this right and
should be the model.

This is also where a three-post site can genuinely win. Nobody is competing for
"how do I get hard water spots off glass" — there is one good answer and almost
no one bothering to give it. Broad phrases are lost before we start.

**Name a service area once, naturally, where the topic is local.** Once. A
neighborhood dropped into every subhead is stuffing, and the study above found
stuffing worthless.

### Where the phrase goes

Pick the one phrase the post is for. Put it in the `title`, in the
`description`, once inside the first 100 words, and in one subhead. That is the
whole placement rule — four spots, stated once each.

Keep the `title` near 60 characters where the sentence allows, so it survives
untruncated in a result, and get the phrase into its first half. The drill post
missed this: "agility drill" was the phrase people search, and it appeared only
in the meta description.

### One scannable block, where the content earns it

A short table or a step list gives both a skimming reader and a retrieval system
a clean, extractable unit. Use one **where the content is genuinely enumerable**
— a procedure with real steps, a comparison with real columns.

Not otherwise. Three bullets manufactured for rhythm is the humanizer skill's
§10, and bolded-header bullets are its §16. The drill post's setup instructions
are a real procedure written as one prose block: that is the case where a list
was owed.

### Freshness

Recency counts, for both readers and answer engines. A seasonal post that will
be true again next year should be **updated rather than rewritten** — set
`updatedAt` in the frontmatter and the page publishes a `dateModified` and an
"Updated" line. The field exists and has never once been used. Refreshing last
autumn's leaf-cleanup post beats writing a near-duplicate, and
[`published.md`](./published.md)'s dedupe rule would block the duplicate anyway.

## Voice

Match the existing posts. Read
`apps/web/content/blog/meet-jackson-the-walker-behind-the-leash-be90e9cf.md`
before writing — it is the reference, not this description of it.

- First-person plural for us ("we", "our students"). Second person for the
  reader ("you", "your block").
- Contractions always. Short sentences. Plain words. A fragment now and then for
  emphasis — but see the fragment rule below, because two in a row is a tell.
- Concrete over abstract: "a golden retriever on the corner", not "pet care
  needs".
- Warm, not folksy. Confident, not salesy. It should read like a neighbor who
  knows the answer, not a brand.

### Sounding like a person

**Read `.agents/skills/humanizer/SKILL.md` and apply it to the draft before the
draft goes anywhere.** It is a 33-pattern catalogue of the specific ways AI
writing gives itself away, built from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" project.
It replaces the short banned-words list this section used to carry, and it is
better than that list was.

This is not optional polish. Gianna rewrites the draft so it doesn't read like a
machine, and that pass is the point of her involvement — but she is fixing our
work, and a draft that arrives already clean is the difference between an editor
and a rewriter.

**Run it in the skill's Embedded mode:** do the draft → audit → final loop
internally and put only the finished prose in the canvas. Never paste its audit
bullets or a summary of changes into the canvas — that is chatbot correspondence
landing in a document Gianna publishes from, which is the skill's own §20.

Every pattern in it applies, but these five are the ones our own drafts have
actually committed, more than once each:

| Pattern | What we did |
|---|---|
| §14 Em dashes | Eleven in the 524-word tutoring post, which went live. The skill bans them outright; see the scope note below. |
| §31 Manufactured punchlines | Every paragraph landing on a tidy closing flourish: *"…which matters more than it sounds like it should."* |
| §9 Negative parallelisms | *"not a full test-prep program, just someone patient"*, then the identical frame in the next week's post. |
| §10 Rule of three | *"easier than shooting, easier than passing, easier than anything…"* |
| §7 AI vocabulary | *"actually"* five times in one post, including in two subheads. It is the first word on the skill's list. |

#### Five places the skill meets a rule of ours

Where they touch, this file wins — but they only genuinely conflict in one spot,
and the rest are scope questions.

1. **The em-dash ban covers published prose only.** No em or en dashes in the
   post body, `title`, `description`, FAQ answers, or `imageAlt`. It does **not**
   apply to this file, `canvas.md`, run logs, Slack messages, or PR text, all of
   which are full of them and stay that way. Do not "fix" our documentation.
2. **The Jackson post is the Voice Calibration sample — and it licenses
   nothing.** The skill says a provided writing sample outranks its style rules
   "including the em dash rule", and instructs you to match the sample's
   frequency. Match it precisely, then: **the body contains zero em dashes.**
   The only one in the file sits in the `description` frontmatter, where it
   introduces a three-item list — so it is an instance of §10 rather than a
   style to copy, and one occurrence in a metadata field establishes no
   frequency to match anyway. There is no conflict here and no grounds to
   reintroduce em dashes. This is written down because the loophole is otherwise
   sitting right there, and a draft looking for permission will find it.
3. **Fragments: one, never two.** This file asks for the occasional fragment and
   the skill's §13 rewrites subjectless ones. Both are satisfied by using one for
   emphasis and never stacking them — a run of clipped fragments is its §31, and
   a fragment tacked on as a tailing negation ("no guessing", "no wasted motion")
   is its §9.
4. **Sentence case for body subheads; post titles keep Title Case.** §17 is about
   headings inside the prose. The `title` frontmatter is a display title and
   follows the existing posts.
5. **Personality yes, claims no.** The skill's PERSONALITY AND SOUL section
   rightly asks for opinion, uncertainty and uneven rhythm, and that is welcome
   here. It also says never to add factual claims to manufacture voice, and on
   this project that is a hard limit rather than a caution: **stance is free,
   claims about our marketplace are not.** "Saturday is a bad day for this and I
   would not fight it" is voice. "Saturdays book up fast" is an invented fact.
   [Honesty rules](#honesty-rules--these-are-hard-limits) govern the difference.

## Honesty rules — these are hard limits

Gianna publishes this under the company's name. A post that invents something is
worse than no post.

1. **Never invent a statistic, a price, or a percentage.** If the useful answer
   needs a real number, write the sentence with a marker instead:
   `[NEEDS REAL NUMBER: typical window-washing price for a 3-bed in Lincoln Park]`.
   Gianna fills it or cuts it. A plausible-sounding invented price is the single
   worst failure this routine can produce.

   ⚠ **This extends past numbers, to any claim about the marketplace you cannot
   check.** Scarcity, demand, popularity, how fast things book, what "most"
   providers charge or "tends to" happen — all of it is invented unless it is in
   this file or the codebase. The first draft this routine ever wrote passed the
   number test and then said *"Tuesday and Thursday at 4pm fill up fast once
   school is in session"* and *"test prep tends to run a little higher."*
   Nobody knows either of those things. With a handful of providers in a
   seven-week pilot, there is no booking pattern to describe.

   The tell: if a sentence asserts something about **the market** rather than
   about **how College Crew works**, and you did not read it in this file or the
   code, cut it or mark it. Writing "we're new, so book early" is honest.
   Writing "slots fill up fast" is not.

   ✅ **Craft knowledge is exempt, and the niche posts depend on it.** "Wash
   windows on an overcast day so the soap doesn't dry before you squeegee it" is
   widely known, checkable, and stable — write it plainly, in as much detail as
   the post needs. The ban is on claims about **our marketplace**: prices,
   demand, scarcity, what most providers charge, how fast things book. A how-to
   post may explain the job in full and still may not name a price.

   Tell them apart by asking **who could check it**. Anyone can check a squeegee
   technique against any other source. Only we could check what our providers
   charge — and we haven't.

   ➕ **The same test says what to reach for, not only what to refuse.** A fact
   anyone can check is not merely permitted, it is the thing most likely to get
   the post quoted by an answer engine — provided you name its source in the
   sentence. See [Cite outward](#cite-outward). A citation you are not certain
   of gets a `[NEEDS REAL SOURCE: …]` marker like any other unverified fact; an
   invented source is worse than none, because it looks checked.
2. **Never invent a named person, customer, student, or pet**, and never write a
   quote nobody said. The named-neighbor detail is what makes these posts good,
   so leave a marker where one belongs:
   `[NEEDS REAL DETAIL: a student who does this — name, school, neighborhood]`.
3. **Never claim a service, coverage area, guarantee, or feature that isn't in
   this file** — no background checks, no insurance claims, no "satisfaction
   guaranteed", no same-day promises — **and never describe what a specific
   booking includes.** See "Niche posts inspire" above: a task list reads as a
   promise even when no promise was intended.
4. **Never name a competitor negatively.** Comparisons describe trade-offs.

Markers are a feature, not a failure — they are how the post gets a real detail
in it instead of a fabricated one. Two or three per post is normal. Collect them
in the canvas under **Needs from you** so Gianna sees them before she reads the
body.

## What the routine hands over

Everything below goes in the standing Slack canvas
(ID in [`canvas.md`](./canvas.md)), which Gianna edits in place before
publishing:

1. **Status** — one line: drafted today, waiting on approval
2. **Approval gate** — **one** checkbox, **unchecked**, worded exactly:

   ```
   * [ ] I approve this blog for production
   ```

   followed by the **Photo** section carrying an empty `Image:` line and the
   upload instruction. Next week's run matches that checkbox string literally
   and reads the `Image:` line for a photo key, so changing either wording
   breaks the gate.

   ⚠ There is **no second checkbox.** A `* [ ] I inserted a photo below the
   line` box was the original design and is gone — pasting the key *is* the
   confirmation. [`canvas.md`](./canvas.md) has the full reasoning. Writing a
   second box back into the canvas would leave Gianna ticking something the
   routine never reads.
3. **Title**
4. **Meta description**
5. **Slug** — lowercase, hyphens, no date
6. **Tags** — the service slug first, then at most two more. In the canvas so
   they are Gianna's to change; they become `keywords` in the structured data
7. **Suggested photo** — what the shot should show, from the team's photoshoot
8. **Caption** — one sentence describing what is in the photo. It becomes the
   image's alt text on the live page: read aloud by screen readers, read by
   search engines, and shown if the image fails to load. Describe the picture,
   don't restate the title.
9. **Needs from you** — **Gianna's to-do list, not yours.** Every `[NEEDS …]`
   marker you left, with where it appears. These are facts you refused to
   invent; she supplies them or cuts the sentence.
10. **Draft** — the body in markdown, editable in place
11. **FAQ** — the q/a pairs
12. **Links** — the internal links in the body and where, then the outbound
    citations with the source each one names
13. **Keep these words** — see below

### "Keep these words"

Gianna rewrites the draft so it doesn't read like AI. That's the point of her
pass, and it will sometimes delete the exact phrase the post was built to rank
for. So end the canvas with the 4–8 phrases carrying the search intent, each
with one line on why it's there:

```
• "window washing in Lincoln Park" — the phrase people search; keep it in the
  title and once in the first two paragraphs
• "how much does it cost" — matches how the question gets typed; keep it as a
  subhead
```

Phrases, not a word list. She can rewrite everything around them.

Now that posts carry outbound citations, add any **source** the post leans on to
this list too — a named authority is exactly the kind of thing a rewrite drops
as clutter, and it is load-bearing.

## The self-check, before the canvas

⚠ **Everything above is only as good as this step.** This file has banned
three-item lists and em-dash pileups since before the first draft was written,
and the tutoring post still went live carrying eleven em dashes in 524 words,
because a rule that is read and never checked is a rule that gets skipped. So the last thing you do before writing the canvas is audit
your own draft, and the audit goes in the run log where someone can see it.

Answer these five in the run log at `docs/agents/runs/<date>-weekly-blog.md`,
briefly and honestly. "Yes" to all five is not the goal — a caught problem you
fixed is a better entry than a clean sweep nobody believes.

1. **The skill's own question, verbatim: "What makes the below so obviously AI
   generated?"** Answer it about your own final draft, not the first one. If the
   honest answer is "nothing", say which two patterns you went looking for.
2. **Em and en dashes in the published prose: how many?** The answer must be
   zero. Scan the body, `title`, `description`, FAQ answers and `imageAlt`
   before you claim it.
3. **Does every section stand alone?** Name any section that opens with a
   pronoun reaching back across its heading, and fix it.
4. **Which outbound sources did you cite, and are you certain each is real?**
   Name them. If none, say why the post had nothing worth sourcing — that is a
   permitted answer, but it should be rare.
5. **Did any sentence assert something about our marketplace that you did not
   read in this file or the code?** Quote it, then cut it or mark it.

If the honest answer to any of these is bad and you cannot fix it, say so in the
canvas under **Needs from you** and in the Slack message. A draft that arrives
with a known flaw named is workable. One that arrives claiming to be clean and
isn't costs Gianna the time she was supposed to be saving.
